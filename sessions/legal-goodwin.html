Legal Session with Goodwin Law Recap · November 4, 2026

Set up your startup the way investors expect

Matt Reardon of Goodwin explains when and where to incorporate, how to issue and vest founder equity, how to make sure your company owns its IP, and how to choose an attorney.

Section 1

Review the Key Takeaways

Use this as a starting point

This recap shares general information from the session. It isn't legal advice. Talk with your own attorney about your company's situation.

Form Your Company

  • Incorporate once you're committed to the idea—when you start pursuing customers, hiring, or raising money. Early on, founders can buy their stock at a very low value, and the company can own the IP from the start.
  • If you plan to raise venture capital or pay employees partly in equity, form a corporation. Most venture capital funds can't invest directly in an LLC, and employees understand stock options.
  • Delaware is still the standard for venture-backed companies (“close to 100%,” per Matt). If you operate elsewhere, expect to pay fees in Delaware and in your home state.

Issue and Split Founder Equity

  • A line in Carta or a spreadsheet isn't issued stock. The board approves the issuance, founders pay for their shares, and a Restricted Stock Purchase Agreement documents it.
  • Split equity by what each founder will contribute over the life of the company, not only what they've done so far. From Matt's experience, an even 50-50 split rarely fits.
  • Put vesting on founder shares. The most common schedule is four years, with 25% vesting after one year and the rest monthly or quarterly. Investors will usually ask for it even if you're a solo founder.

Own and Protect Your IP

  • Have everyone who works on the company sign an NDA and an Assignment of Inventions Agreement, including early collaborators. Investors will ask you to prove it.

Choose an Attorney

  • Look for someone who works with early-stage companies in your industry. Then choose someone you trust and work well with.

Section 2

Review the Founder Questions

Choosing Your Entity

What kind of entity should I form? I'm used to LLCs.

Matt uses two questions. First, will you raise money from investors, especially venture capitalists? Second, will you hire people and pay them partly in equity? If either answer is yes, a corporation almost always makes sense. Equity in an LLC (a profits interest) makes people partners in the LLC rather than employees, and most candidates don't know how it works. An LLC can fit if you plan to run the business on your own.

Can I convert my LLC to a corporation later?

Yes. Matt noted that the reverse doesn't work, so plan for a one-way move. The more people hold equity in your LLC, and the more classes of equity you issue, the harder the conversion gets.

My startup sits under an LLC parent company I own. Should I spin it out?

Let your investors' needs drive the structure. Investors often don't want to buy into a partially owned subsidiary, because a parent company could divert proceeds or sale opportunities. If that's what you hear from investors, plan to move the startup out from under the parent.

Should I incorporate in Wyoming or Texas instead?

Matt sees those as special cases for a few high-profile companies that wanted more rights for controlling stockholders. Those states don't have Delaware's body of case law, so investors still want their portfolio companies in Delaware.

Issuing Equity

Our founder split lives in a spreadsheet. How do I make it official?

File a charter in Delaware that sets the number of shares. Have the board approve the issuance in a meeting or by written consent. Have each founder pay for their shares and sign a Restricted Stock Purchase Agreement with vesting. Then record it in Carta or your spreadsheet. This paper trail matters in your data room, for capital gains holding periods if you sell, and for the qualified small business stock exemption if you qualify. Goodwin's Founders Workbench and Stripe Atlas both offer the form documents.

We haven't raised money yet. Do we need outside board members?

You need a board of directors at every stage, but it can be just the founders. With a near-even split, both founders usually sit on the board. When one CEO holds significantly more shares, often only the CEO does.

How do I adjust equity as people's contributions change?

You can't take shares away from someone who stays, but the balance shifts over time. Each funding round usually expands the equity incentive pool, which dilutes earlier holders. New contributors then get stock options at the company's current fair market value, so their equity grows only if the company does.

How do I give equity to people helping before I can pay them?

There are two ways. You can sell them common stock at fair market value (the board's informed, good-faith judgment) with vesting. If they pay less, they owe tax on stock they can't sell. Or you can grant stock options, which they don't pay for until they exercise. Options need a 409A valuation so the exercise price is at least fair market value. You can also treat their services as payment for shares, but you still need to value both. If they're employees, they must be paid as employees.

Funding Your Company

Should founders put their own money in through a SAFE, a loan, or by buying stock?

Not by buying stock, because that puts a value on the company. A SAFE converts into preferred stock, which founders may not want. Matt's most common approach is a simple one-page demand loan at the lowest allowable interest rate. Repayment isn't guaranteed. New investors often want every dollar to go into the company. If they won't repay it in cash, you can ask to convert the loan into the same preferred stock they're buying.

I'm raising from angel investors in my field, not VCs. What could I get wrong?

Matt's guidance: raise only from accredited investors, and have each one confirm their status. Stay within an exemption from registering your securities, which means no public offering and a limited group of investors. Comply with state securities laws as well as federal ones. Don't promise any returns. Securities rules are detailed, so review your plans with an attorney before you raise.

Section 3

Apply This to Your Venture

  • Incorporate once you're committed to pursuing customers, hires, or funding.
  • If you plan to raise venture capital, form a Delaware corporation.
  • Have your board approve founder stock, pay for your shares, and sign a Restricted Stock Purchase Agreement with vesting.
  • Have everyone who has worked on the company sign an NDA and an Assignment of Inventions Agreement.
  • Keep board consents, payment records, and signed agreements together in your data room.
  • Get a 409A valuation before you grant stock options.
  • Find an attorney who works with early-stage companies in your industry.
