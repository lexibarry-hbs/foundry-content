Pitch the problem before the product

Section 1

Review the Key Takeaways

Tell a Story Investors Can Feel

  • Lead with a specific, urgent problem. Firas calls it making investors wear a “coat of pain”: show what the problem looks like day to day for the person who has it, before you describe your product.
  • Build your problem statement in four parts: who struggles and how, why it’s hard today, how you solve it, and the outcome. The outcome is usually measured in money and time saved.
  • Go narrow, not broad. “Claims processing is broken” is easy to agree with and hard to act on. “Clinics lose 15% of revenue from denied claims caused by human error” (Firas’s example) is specific and checkable.

Build a Story That Holds Up

  • At the earliest stage, your story is what investors examine. Expect them to check your numbers and talk to customers, or people like them. Claims that hold up build your credibility as the right founder.
  • Investors look for four things: founder-market fit, proof of pain, why now, and a credible path to a big outcome. From Firas’s experience, why now matters less than the first two.
  • Keep building during a raise. Diligence often takes 3 to 6 weeks (as shared in the session), so a new pilot, a bigger pilot scope, or a converted customer between meetings shows momentum.
  • Be truthful. Investors verify traction and market sizing through their own networks. A claim that doesn’t hold up, like a pilot you don’t have, invites extra scrutiny.

Know Your Ask

  • Raise to a milestone, not a number. Name the hires, product version, or customers the money unlocks.
  • Venture capital isn’t the only path. Revenue from customers, bootstrapping, grants, accelerators, and angels can fit better, depending on your goals. Explore Foundry Funding content to compare your options.
  • Prepare a 30-second, three-minute, and 10-minute version of your pitch, and practice the short one most.
Section 2

Show Signal Before Revenue

What investors look for at the pre-revenue stage depends on your business model.

B2B SaaSLetters of intent, paid pilots, and testimonials from early design customers
MarketplacesRepeat transactions and retention on both the buyer and seller side
FreemiumEngagement (daily and monthly active users), willingness to pay for premium features, and customer feedback
Hardware, deep tech, and healthcareMilestones such as SOC 2 or HIPAA compliance, grants, regulatory wins, and pilots in real settings
Section 3

Avoid the Reasons Investors Say No

  • The problem is real but not urgent
  • It’s unclear why your team is the one to win
  • The market looks too small for a venture-scale outcome
  • Traction claims don’t match the evidence
  • The ask isn’t tied to a milestone
Section 4

Follow the Pitch Arc

1ProblemWho it hurts and how badly
2Why nowYour solution and why the timing works
3ProofYour research and early signal
4MarketHow big this gets if you win
5TeamWhy you’re the right people
6AskThe amount and the milestone it unlocks
Section 5

Prepare Three Versions of Your Pitch

30 secondsYour elevator pitch for networking events: the problem, its size, how you solve it, and what success could look like.
Three minutesAdd why you, your early results, and your ask. Aim for one or two “aha” moments.
10 minutesThe full pitch arc, usually with a deck: problem, solution, market, competition, traction, next milestones, and your ask.

Practice all three versions in the Pitch Simulator.

Run your raise like a sales pipeline

Lean on warm introductions over cold emails, track every investor conversation, and follow up with progress. “We just signed two more pilots” warms up an investor who said it was too early.

Section 6

Choose the Right Funding Path

BootstrappingFund growth yourself until you know exactly what outside money would unlock.
Revenue from customersThe strongest validation there is: people who care enough about the problem to pay you to solve it. Everything else is a promise that those customers exist. That’s why investors weigh annual recurring revenue (ARR) and net revenue retention (NRR) above most other startup metrics.
GrantsNon-dilutive funding from federal programs such as SBIR and STTR, the NIH, and other research institutions.
AcceleratorsStructure for your build, plus a signal to later-stage investors.
AngelsEarly checks, often from about $5,000 to $100,000 each, as shared in the session. Many angels spread small checks across many companies.
Venture capitalFor companies that could reach a venture-scale outcome. Firas described early rounds as typically 10% to 20% dilution, plus regular reporting to investors and your board.

Figures are as shared in the session and vary by investor and program.

Section 7

Get Answers to Founder Questions

I don’t have a technical cofounder yet. Will investors still back my team?

At the early stage, traction and knowing your blind spots matter most. Name the gap and your plan to fill it—for example, the technical cofounder who will take your prototype to a full product. From Firas’s experience, heading off an investor’s questions this way builds credibility, because truthfulness wins at this stage.

How do I share a big vision without overpromising?

Center your pitch on the next milestone. When you size the market, frame it as the world where you win: if everything goes to plan, here’s how big this gets. Firas noted that in his investing career, only one company ever matched its projections to actual revenue for a quarter, so investors read projections as a vision, not a promise.

What stops a bigger company with more resources from building this first?

The most compelling answer Firas heard as an investor was speed. Large companies carry technical debt and slow decision-making, and they haven’t built it yet. He noted that investors also weigh acquisition as a possible exit, but he advised against raising that yourself when you pitch.

What should I cut from a shorter pitch? Is too much backstory a common mistake?

Keep just enough backstory to make the problem feel uncomfortable and unresolved. Then release that tension with your solution. In 30 seconds, cover the problem, its size, how you solve it, and the outcome. In three minutes, add why you, early results, and your ask. Save the full arc for the 10-minute version.

How do I choose between grants, bootstrapping, angels, accelerators, and VC?

Consider venture capital when the problem is big enough that you need institutional support, and you’re convinced it can be huge. It usually means giving up 10% to 20% of your company and reporting to investors every quarter. Less dilutive paths give you room to test whether your idea is venture-scale. From Firas’s experience, a strong moment to raise is when demand grows faster than you can keep up.

Section 8

Apply This to Your Venture

  • Write your problem statement in four lines: who struggles, why it’s hard today, how you solve it, and what it saves in money and time. Read the Customer Problem Section, then draft yours with Problem Statement AI.
  • Replace one broad claim in your deck with a specific number you can source.
  • List the signal you have for your business model, and what you could add in the next six weeks.
  • Map your deck to the pitch arc: problem, why now, proof, market, team, and ask.
  • Write your ask as a milestone: the hires, product version, or customers the money unlocks.
  • Practice your 30-second pitch until it’s easy. Then practice the three-minute and 10-minute versions with Foundry AI.
